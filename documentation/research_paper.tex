\begin{abstract}
A faulty sensor in an automatic weather station quietly corrupts every forecast and alert built on
it, yet a genuine heat wave or storm can look just as unusual as a broken instrument. We study this
problem on one year of hourly ERA5 reanalysis for eight Indian cities (280{,}320 readings over four
sensors), augmented with six injected sensor-fault types and four seasonal, physically coherent
extreme-weather types. Four unsupervised detectors (Isolation Forest, Local Outlier Factor, k-Means
distance and PCA reconstruction error) vote on every reading, and a cross-sensor coherence rule
separates faults from extremes. On its own this unsupervised stage reaches 91.8\% accuracy, 49.8\%
balanced accuracy and a macro F1 of 0.528 on the normal/fault/extreme task. Adding a small
supervised Random Forest booster, which takes the detector scores as inputs and is trained on the
injected labels, raises these to 97.9\%, 82.4\% and 0.876 under station-grouped cross-validation. We
report the majority-class baseline (93.0\%) and an operational range check alongside every figure.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
unsupervised learning, anomaly detection, isolation forest, sensor faults, weather stations,
random forest.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Automatic weather stations feed numerical forecasts, agricultural advisories and disaster warnings.
When a sensor fails loudly, for example by reporting a temperature of 200\,\textdegree C, a fixed
range check catches it. Most failures are quieter: a sensor sticks at one value, drifts by a few
degrees over a week, or loses precision. These readings stay inside physical limits and pass
unnoticed.

The opposite error matters just as much. A heat wave pushes temperature far above its seasonal
level, and a check that flags every unusual reading would discard exactly the data that disaster
response needs. A useful system must answer two questions for every reading: \emph{is it unusual},
and \emph{is the instrument at fault or is the weather genuinely extreme}.

Labelled failure logs are rarely available, which makes unsupervised anomaly detection the natural
starting point. The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a benchmark built by injecting realistic faults and seasonal extremes into one year of real
ERA5 weather for eight climatically diverse Indian stations;
\item an unsupervised pipeline of four classical detectors combined by voting, with a cross-sensor
coherence rule that separates faults from extremes;
\item a measurement of how much a small supervised booster, stacked on the unsupervised scores,
improves the result when labelled examples are available.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Sensor faults in environmental networks have been catalogued by Ni \emph{et al.}~\cite{ni2009} and
measured in real deployments by Sharma \emph{et al.}~\cite{sharma2010}, who identify short spikes,
constant (stuck) readings and noise as the most common types. Operational meteorology relies on
rule-based quality control such as range, step and persistence tests~\cite{wmo2004,durre2010}. These
rules are transparent but cannot tell a real extreme from a fault.

Unsupervised anomaly detection~\cite{chandola2009} includes isolation-based methods~\cite{liu2008},
density-based methods such as Local Outlier Factor~\cite{breunig2000}, clustering distance and
subspace reconstruction error. Combining several detectors improves robustness~\cite{aggarwal2017}.
When some labels exist, stacking a supervised learner on unsupervised outlier scores is an
established way to improve detection~\cite{micenkova2014}, and tree ensembles such as Random
Forests~\cite{breiman2001} are a strong default for tabular data~\cite{grinsztajn2022}. Because
labelled failure logs are rarely published, fault injection into clean data is the standard way to
build benchmarks~\cite{sharma2010}.

\section{Data}
\subsection{Weather readings}
Hourly temperature, relative humidity, 10\,m wind speed and mean sea-level pressure for 2023 were
taken from the Open-Meteo archive~\cite{openmeteo}, which serves ERA5 reanalysis~\cite{hersbach2020}.
The eight stations (Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, Hyderabad, Jaipur, Kolkata, Bengaluru, Shimla) cover
arid, coastal, tropical, inland and hill climates. Each station has 8{,}760 hours, giving
280{,}320 sensor readings. Gaps of at most two hours were linearly interpolated before injection, so
the only missing values left are injected dropouts.

\subsection{Injected faults}
Faults affect one sensor at a time, as a real failing instrument does. Each station receives five
episodes of each type at random, non-overlapping times. With $\sigma$ the standard deviation of the
sensor at that station:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{bias}: a constant offset of $4$--$7\sigma$ for 24--72\,h;
\item \emph{drift}: an offset growing linearly from $1.5\sigma$ to $5$--$8\sigma$ over 3--7 days;
\item \emph{stuck}: the first value repeated for 12--48\,h;
\item \emph{spike}: about 15\% of hours in a window offset by $6$--$10\sigma$;
\item \emph{precision}: rounding to a step of $0.8$--$1.5\sigma$ plus Gaussian noise;
\item \emph{dropout}: missing readings for 6--24\,h.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Injected extremes}
Extremes move several sensors together and occur only in their usual season. Each station receives
two of each. The effect follows a half-sine bump over the event, and only hours above 30\% of the
peak are labelled, because the edges are indistinguishable from normal weather. Heat waves
(April--June) raise temperature by about 11\,\textdegree C and lower humidity and pressure; cold
snaps (December--February) lower temperature and raise wind and humidity; storms (June--October)
raise wind and humidity and drop pressure by about 14\,hPa; heavy rain (June--September) saturates
humidity and cools the air.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Features}
Every feature is computed per station and sensor from the measured values only. Missing values are
forward-filled for the arithmetic and counted separately. Windows are centred, since the system
analyses a completed record. Table~\ref{tab:features} lists the sixteen features; the unsupervised
detectors use the fourteen numeric ones, leaving out sensor identity and hour.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Features computed for every reading}
\label{tab:features}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.2cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
Feature & Definition \\
\midrule
value\_z & reading standardised by station and sensor \\
dev\_day & reading minus 24\,h median, in $\sigma$ \\
dev\_week & 24\,h median minus 14-day median, in $\sigma$ \\
jump & change from the previous hour, in $\sigma$ \\
noise\_6h, noise\_day & std of hourly changes over 6\,h and 24\,h \\
flat\_6h & identical consecutive readings in 6\,h \\
missing\_6h & missing readings in 6\,h \\
others\_dev, others\_week & mean $|$dev\_day$|$ and $|$dev\_week$|$ of the other three sensors \\
temp/humid/wind/ press\_week & dev\_week of each sensor at the same station and hour \\
sensor\_code, hour & sensor identity and hour of day \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Unsupervised detectors}
The numeric features are standardised, and four detectors from scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011}
are fitted on a random sample of 20{,}000 readings without any label:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Isolation Forest}~\cite{liu2008} with 200 trees; the score is the negative mean path
length;
\item \emph{Local Outlier Factor}~\cite{breunig2000} with 20 neighbours in novelty mode;
\item \emph{k-Means} with 8 clusters; the score is the distance to the nearest centroid;
\item \emph{PCA} keeping 90\% of the variance (11 components); the score is the squared
reconstruction error.
\end{itemize}
Each detector votes for a reading when its score lies in its top 5\%. A reading with at least two
of four votes is flagged. A flagged reading is labelled an \emph{extreme} when the other sensors of
the same station are also moving (others\_week $> 0.5\sigma$) and a \emph{fault} otherwise, because a
failing instrument moves alone while weather moves several sensors together.

\subsection{Supervised booster}
The unsupervised stage cannot learn which pattern of scores corresponds to which class. When
labelled examples are available, a small supervised model can add this. We stack a Random
Forest~\cite{breiman2001} with 100 trees, maximum depth 20 and at least two samples per leaf on top
of the detectors: its inputs are the sixteen features, the four detector scores and the vote count,
and it predicts one of the eleven classes. This follows the idea of learning from unsupervised
outlier scores~\cite{micenkova2014}.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
Neighbouring hours are nearly identical, so a random row split would place almost the same reading
in both training and test sets. The booster is therefore evaluated with 4-fold GroupKFold using the
station as the group: each fold trains on six stations and predicts the remaining
two~\cite{roberts2017}, so every reading receives an out-of-station prediction.

Because the classes are imbalanced~\cite{he2009}, we report accuracy with balanced accuracy (mean
per-class recall) and macro F1. Two baselines are used: always predicting \emph{normal}, and an
operational range check that labels a reading as a fault when it is missing or outside fixed limits
(temperature $-5$ to $45$\,\textdegree C, humidity 0--100\%, wind 0--120\,km/h, pressure
950--1050\,hPa).

\section{Results}
\subsection{Overall performance}
Table~\ref{tab:main} compares the stages. The unsupervised detectors improve balanced accuracy over
the range check (49.8\% against 44.2\%) and macro F1 (0.528 against 0.485), but they flag 5.2\% of
all readings, so their plain accuracy (91.8\%) falls below the majority baseline. Adding the
supervised booster raises accuracy to 97.9\%, balanced accuracy to 82.4\% and macro F1 to 0.876. On
the full eleven-class task the boosted model reaches 97.3\% accuracy and a macro F1 of 0.773.

For the boosted model, per-station accuracy ranges from 97.4\% (Jaipur) to 98.5\% (Mumbai), and
per-station macro F1 from 0.837 to 0.905, so performance carries over to climates the booster did not
train on.

\subsection{Confusion between groups}
Table~\ref{tab:cm} shows the three-group confusion matrices. The unsupervised stage finds 39.1\% of
fault readings but only 13.7\% of extreme readings, and raises alarms on 3.4\% of normal readings.
The booster cuts false alarms to 0.3\% and lifts recall to 83.2\% for faults and 64.3\% for
extremes. Its main remaining error is missed extremes, mostly from the shoulders of long events and
from short heavy-rain episodes.

\subsection{Episodes}
Consecutive flagged hours on one sensor, with gaps of at most three hours, are merged into episodes
for the dashboard. The boosted model produces 879 episodes (594 fault, 285 extreme), and 80.3\% of
them carry the correct group.

\section{Discussion}
The unsupervised detectors do what they are designed for: they rank unusual readings without any
label and already beat the range check on balanced accuracy and macro F1. Their weakness is the
decision step. A fixed vote threshold flags many unusual but healthy readings, and a single
coherence rule cannot capture the different signatures of the ten event types. The booster learns
exactly this decision from labelled examples, and the importance analysis shows that it still draws
on the detector scores.

Accuracy alone is a weak summary here: a model that never flags anything already scores 93.0\%,
higher than the unsupervised stage. Balanced accuracy and macro F1 show the real progression from
range check to unsupervised detectors to the boosted model.

\section{Limitations}
The faults and extremes are injected, so their magnitudes and shapes are authored, and real failures
may be subtler. The booster is trained on these injected labels, so the boosted figures should be
read as an upper bound for a live network until the model is retrained on logged failures. The vote
and coherence thresholds of the unsupervised stage were chosen by comparing a small grid of values
against the labels, so even that stage is not entirely free of label information. The weather itself
is reanalysis rather than raw station data, which is smoother than real observations, and centred
windows use a few future hours, which suits offline auditing but not real-time alerts.

\section{Conclusion}
Four classical unsupervised detectors, combined by voting and a cross-sensor coherence rule, detect
sensor faults and weather extremes without labels, reaching 91.8\% accuracy, 49.8\% balanced
accuracy and a macro F1 of 0.528 across eight Indian stations. Adding a small supervised Random
Forest booster on top of the detector scores raises performance to 97.9\% accuracy, 82.4\% balanced
accuracy and a macro F1 of 0.876 under station-grouped cross-validation. Future work will add real
station observations, logged instrument failures and trailing-window features for online
monitoring.
